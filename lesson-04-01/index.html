Part A · Build the ideas yourself

Discuss your answer with a partner before checking. Some questions deliberately introduce material developed further in 4.2.

1
What does the word “momentum” mean in everyday life? How might its scientific meaning be more precise?
Think of two moving objects with different masses and speeds.
Worked answer: In physics, linear momentum is a measurable vector quantity: mass multiplied by velocity. Everyday usage often means “progress” or “impetus,” which is less precise.
2
What is meant by linear momentum?
Recall that linear describes translational rather than rotational motion.
Worked answer: The momentum associated with translational motion, defined by p⃗ = m v⃗. Unless specified otherwise, “momentum” means linear momentum in this unit.
3
What is the defining equation for the momentum of an object?
Which known property of matter and quantity describing motion could be multiplied?
Worked answer: p⃗ = m v⃗, where m is mass in kg and v⃗ is velocity in m/s.
4
What are the SI units of momentum?
Use p = mv; there is no special named SI unit for momentum.
Worked answer: kg·m/s. This is equivalent to N·s because 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
5
Is momentum a vector or scalar? What direction does it have?
Mass is scalar; velocity is vector.
Worked answer: Momentum is a vector. Since mass is positive, p⃗ points in the same direction as v⃗.
6
A 1200 kg car travels east at 15 m/s. How does its momentum compare with a 600 kg car travelling east at 30 m/s?
Compare the products m × v, not just speeds.
Worked answer: Both have momentum 18 000 kg·m/s east. Equal momentum does not mean equal kinetic energy.
7
What does the gradient of a momentum–time graph represent?
For constant mass, p = mv. Recall the gradient of a velocity–time graph and Fnet = ma.
Worked answer: Gradient Δp/Δt gives the net force (or instantaneous net force from a tangent for a curved p–t graph). It is signed in one dimension.
8
How can you calculate the change in an object’s momentum?
Change means final minus initial; do not subtract magnitudes when directions differ.
Worked answer: Δp⃗ = p⃗ − p⃗0. Graphically add p⃗ to (−p⃗0), or draw Δp⃗ from the tip of p⃗0 to the tip of p⃗.
9
What is the name given to the change in momentum caused by a net force over a time interval?
The name starts with I.
Worked answer: The net impulse imparted to the object: J⃗net = Δp⃗. Its SI units are N·s, equivalent to kg·m/s.
10
What does the signed area under an acceleration–time graph represent?
a = Δv/Δt. Imagine a rectangle with units (m/s²) × s.
Worked answer: The signed change in velocity Δv. Above the axis contributes positive Δv; below contributes negative Δv.
11
What does the signed area under a net force–time graph represent?
Use Fnet = ma for a constant-mass object, and your previous answer.
Worked answer: The net impulse, equal to change in momentum: area = Jnet = Δp. A curved graph requires estimating/summing areas; calculus is not needed here.
12
What is the difference between a collision and an explosion in the system model?
Consider whether the interacting objects approach/contact, or are pushed apart by internal forces.
Worked answer: A collision models a short interaction whose forces between objects dominate external forces. An explosion models internal forces driving objects of a system apart. Detailed conservation calculations come in 4.3.

Part B · Three guided numerical examples

Example 1 · SI units

2.5 kg object at +4.0 m/s

p = mv

p = (2.5)(+4.0) = +10 kg·m/s

Example 2 · Non-SI mass

200 g ball at −6.0 m/s

m = 0.200 kg

p = (0.200)(−6.0) = −1.20 kg·m/s

Example 3 · Rearrangement

Momentum +18 kg·m/s, mass 3.0 kg

v = p/m

v = (+18)/(3.0) = +6.0 m/s

Part D · Momentum-change practice

Eight problems, ranging from signed one-dimensional momentum to 2D vector subtraction. Work them before checking.

V1
A 0.40 kg cart travels at +3.0 m/s. Find p.
p = mv; show direction.
Worked answer: p = (0.40 kg)(+3.0 m/s) = +1.20 kg·m/s.
V2
A 150 g ball moves west at 8.0 m/s. Find its momentum using east as positive.
Convert grams to kg; west is negative.
Worked answer: m = 0.150 kg; p = (0.150)(−8.0) = −1.20 kg·m/s (west).
V3
A trolley has momentum +12 kg·m/s and mass 3.0 kg. Determine its velocity.
Rearrange p = mv.
Worked answer: v = p/m = (+12)/(3.0) = +4.0 m/s.
V4
A toy car moves at −2.5 m/s and has momentum −0.75 kg·m/s. Determine its mass.
Rearrange p = mv; negative signs cancel.
Worked answer: m = p/v = (−0.75)/(−2.5) = 0.30 kg.
V5
A ball changes momentum from +4 to −3 kg·m/s. Find Δp.
Final minus initial; do not just find a difference in magnitudes.
Worked answer: Δp = −3 − (+4) = −7 kg·m/s.
V6
A ball has initial momentum −6 kg·m/s and gains an impulse of +10 N·s. Find final momentum.
p = p0 + Δp.
Worked answer: p = −6 + 10 = +4 kg·m/s.
V7
In the plane, p⃗0 = (3, 1) and p⃗ = (−1, 4) kg·m/s. Determine Δp⃗ and its magnitude.
Subtract x-components and y-components separately.
Worked answer: Δp⃗ = (−1−3, 4−1) = (−4, +3) kg·m/s. |Δp| = √(16+9) = 5 kg·m/s.
V8
An object experiences Δp⃗ = (+2, −5) kg·m/s. Its initial momentum is (−3,+1) kg·m/s. Find final momentum.
Add Δp⃗ to p⃗0 component by component.
Worked answer: p⃗ = (−3+2, 1−5) = (−1, −4) kg·m/s.

Part E · Mixed practice (including graphs)

The first questions consolidate 4.1; the later impulse and force–time questions provide a first look at 4.2, without extended impulse applications.

M1
Two moving objects: A has m = 2.0 kg and v = +3.0 m/s; B has m = 4.0 kg and v = −1.5 m/s. Compare their momenta.
Sign indicates direction; distinguish magnitude from value.
Worked answer: pA = +6.0 kg·m/s; pB = −6.0 kg·m/s. Equal magnitudes, opposite directions.
M2
A 0.20 kg ball moves east at 10 m/s, then west at 5.0 m/s. Find its change in momentum.
Choose east positive and calculate each momentum first.
Worked answer: p0 = +2.0; p = −1.0; Δp = −3.0 kg·m/s (west).
M3
In a p–t graph, momentum rises from 0 to +12 kg·m/s uniformly over 3 s. Determine net force.
Gradient = rise/run.
Worked answer: Fnet = Δp/Δt = (+12−0)/(3−0) = +4 N.
M4
In a p–t graph, momentum remains +5 kg·m/s between 1 s and 4 s. What is the net force in that interval?
What is the slope of a horizontal line?
Worked answer: Slope = 0, so Fnet = 0 N. The object can still be moving.
M5
A p–t graph descends uniformly from +6 kg·m/s at 0 s to −6 kg·m/s at 4 s. Calculate net force.
Use signed gradient; what does crossing p = 0 mean?
Worked answer: Fnet = (−6−6)/4 = −3 N. Momentum becomes zero at t = 2 s.
M6
A p–t graph curves upwards (becomes steeper). What happens to the net force?
A changing slope means a changing force.
Worked answer: The net force in the positive direction increases: the instantaneous gradient becomes more positive.
M7
A net F–t graph is a constant +6 N from t = 0 to 0.50 s. Find the impulse.
Signed rectangle area = F × Δt.
Worked answer: J = (+6)(0.50) = +3.0 N·s = +3.0 kg·m/s.
M8
A net F–t graph is a triangle from 0 to 0.40 s with peak +10 N. Find the impulse.
Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height.
Worked answer: J = ½(0.40 s)(10 N) = +2.0 N·s.
M9
A net F–t graph has +4 N for 2 s, then −3 N for 2 s. Find the overall impulse.
Add signed areas, not absolute areas.
Worked answer: J = (+4)(2) + (−3)(2) = +2 N·s.
M10
A ball’s momentum changes from −5 to +7 kg·m/s in 0.030 s. Find average net force.
First find Δp.
Worked answer: Δp = +7 − (−5) = +12 kg·m/s; Favg,net = 12/0.030 = +400 N.
M11
Two carts collide briefly. During the collision, why may we initially neglect the external forces when defining a system containing both carts?
Compare the interaction forces with the net external force over the brief collision.
Worked answer: The forces between the carts can be much larger than the net external forces during the short interaction. It is then a useful collision model; conservation is developed in 4.3.
M12
A stationary firework bursts into two pieces. What type of interaction is this, and what causes the pieces to separate?
Which forces are internal to the chosen system?
Worked answer: An explosion: internal forces between parts of the system push the pieces apart. Do not assume each piece has zero momentum after the burst.
